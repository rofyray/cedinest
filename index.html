Building CediNest: dollar savings counted in cedis

A Nimiq Pay mini app for Ghana. Your USDT goes into Aave on Polygon, in your own name, and every balance, goal and gain is shown in Ghana cedis. Here is how it works, what nearly broke it, and what we learned.

Why this exists

Money saved in cedis loses value while it sits. Over the last few years the cedi has swung hard against the dollar, so the people with the clearest reason to save, for rent advance or school fees, are the ones most exposed. In Ghana, landlords commonly ask for one or two years of rent up front, so a rent goal is big, slow and easy to lose to the exchange rate.

Many people here already know the answer: hold dollars. What they lack is a savings habit around it that speaks their language. Prices in Ghana are in cedis, and so is rent. The mental maths of "how much of my rent advance do I have" happens in cedis. A dollar balance on its own does not answer that question.

CediNest does the conversion for you, in both directions. You save in dollars and watch the goal fill up in cedis. The savings earn a variable rate in Aave. And the stamp card, borrowed from the susu collector's card that generations of Ghanaians have saved with, makes each small deposit feel like progress.

What it is

CediNest runs inside Nimiq Pay as a mini app. Nimiq Pay gives it two accounts: a NIM account and a Polygon account that holds the user's USDT. A save moves USDT from that account into Aave v3 on Polygon, supplied on behalf of the user, so the interest-bearing aToken lands in their own wallet. A take-out does the reverse. CediNest never holds the money. It holds the goals, the stamp history and a record of each job, in a small Postgres database.

The hard part: nobody has POL

Most Nimiq Pay users hold NIM and USDT. Nobody holds POL, the gas token on Polygon, and a mini app cannot sponsor gas on the user's behalf. Without a solution, the very first save fails with an error about gas that a saver in Kumasi has no reason to understand.

So the user never sends a Polygon transaction at all. Instead:

  1. The app asks the API for a quote. The API prices the Polygon gas from live gas, POL and NIM prices, applies a 50 NIM floor, and stores a quote that expires in ten minutes.
  2. Approval one. The user pays the fee in NIM (about one cent) from Nimiq Pay, with the quote id in the transaction memo.
  3. Approval two. The user signs an EIP-2612 permit for exactly the amount being saved. Nimiq Pay shows the typed data, and the app checks locally that the recovered signer is the user before it sends anything.
  4. The API verifies the NIM payment on the Nimiq chain, then a relayer wallet calls our contract with the permit, and the contract supplies to Aave with onBehalfOf = user.

Two taps in Nimiq Pay and no POL. The money goes straight from the user's wallet into their own Aave position.

The relay contract is deliberately boring

CediNestRelay.sol is about seventy lines. It exposes exactly two functions, depositFor and withdrawFor, both callable only by the relayer, and both can only move a user's money between that user's wallet and that user's own Aave position. The owner can rotate the relayer and nothing else. There is no admin withdraw, no upgrade path and no fee taken in USDT.

Threat model in one sentence: a stolen relayer key can, at worst, move a user's savings between their own Aave position and their own wallet, and only with a valid permit from that user. It cannot take them.

A permit that someone front-runs still works, because the contract falls back to the existing allowance. Fork tests against live Polygon cover the happy paths, withdraw-all after thirty days of interest, bad and expired and reused signatures, the front-run case, and relayer rotation. The contract is live at 0xA46C…f4E4.

Gotcha one: USDT on Polygon is not the USDT you think

Since the August 2025 in-place upgrade, USDT on Polygon is USDT0. The token still shows as USDT in wallets, but its EIP-712 domain for permits is non-standard: the name is USDT0, the version is 1, the verifying contract is the token, and instead of a chainId field it carries a salt equal to the chain id (137) as bytes32. The field order matters too: name, version, verifyingContract, salt.

// shared/constants.ts
export const USDT0_DOMAIN = {
  name: 'USDT0', version: '1',
  verifyingContract: USDT0,
  salt: '0x0000…0089',   // chain id 137, no chainId field
}

Sign with the standard domain and the recovered signer is simply wrong. The error does not say "wrong domain", it says the permit is invalid, which sends you hunting in the wrong place. The aToken, aPolUSDT, uses the standard domain. Both are checked against DOMAIN_SEPARATOR() on mainnet by a script before anything else runs.

Gotcha two: Nimiq Pay keeps your NIM somewhere else

The first real recording of the demo died on a refusal: "Not enough NIM for the stamp fee. You have 0 NIM." The phone had almost thirty thousand NIM.

Nimiq Pay holds a user's spendable NIM in a hashed time-locked contract shared with its cosigner, which is what gives its payments instant finality. The wallet address a mini app sees first "usually reads 0". The balance lives at a second address, the current HTLC, which Nimiq Pay replaces about every week and after top-ups. The fix was in three parts: read the balance across every account the SDK lists, refresh on the accountsChanged event, and never refuse a payment on the app's own reading. Nimiq Pay decides whether it can pay the fee. The reading only explains a refusal after the fact.

Gotcha three: when is a NIM payment final?

The API only relays once the fee is paid, so it has to know when a NIM transaction is final. Nimiq finality is by macro block, so the verifier checks the memo, the amount, the recipient and the execution result, then waits until the transaction's block is at or before the last macro block. The job states are deliberately explicit:

fee_pending → fee_final → submitted → done
                                   ↘ failed      (bad fee payment, nothing moved)
                                   ↘ refund_due  (fee paid, relay failed or the user cancelled the signature)

If the user cancels the signature after paying the fee, the app files the job anyway so the fee shows up in a daily refund list. Every error message says what happened to the money, because that is the only question a saver has at that moment.

Lessons

Show the money in the unit people think in

Every number on screen is in cedis first and dollars second. The chart on the home screen draws two lines: your dollar savings counted in cedis, and the same money if you had kept it in cedis. When the cedi falls, the gap is the point of the app. When the cedi gains, the caption says so plainly. We would rather show an honest dip than a chart that only goes up.

Always say what happened to the money

Every error in the app ends with the state of the user's funds: "Nothing moved", "Your USDT has not moved", "We will send your 50 NIM back within 24 hours". A technical cause helps us. The saver needs to know where the money is.

Check the feasibility pieces before writing the app

Three things could have sunk the project: whether a salt-domain permit lets a second account supply to Aave on your behalf, whether Nimiq Pay would sign that typed data on a real phone, and whether a NIM payment with a memo could be verified from a server. A hidden lab page signed both permits and paid ten NIM from a real phone before any screen was designed.

Liquidity is a product constraint

Getting NIM and USDT into a Ghanaian test wallet took longer than writing the relay contract. Several swap services list NIM but will not fill an order, minimums were too high, and the web wallet's USDT path was under maintenance that week. The 50 NIM fee floor (about one cent) exists partly so that a few hundred NIM lasts a saver a long time.

Borrow the habit and skip the jargon

Nobody in the app is asked to think about aTokens, permits or gas. They stamp a saving, the stamp office does the rest, and when it lands the screen says "E Enter!". The susu card is doing the work that a tutorial would otherwise have to do.

What is next

  • Goal archive becomes a real delete when a goal has no stamps.
  • Reminders tied to the stamp card: a nudge on the days you usually stamp.
  • Moving contract ownership to a cold address, and publishing the refund log.
